## What they did

1. Trained a diffusion autoencoder without labels on 12,019 sagittal 2D CT slices of vertebrae,
   96 × 96 pixels; semantic latent of 512 values per image.
2. Trained a linear classifier (linear layer or support vector machine) on the frozen latent,
   healthy (grade 0) versus fractured (grades 2 and 3). Grade 1 was left out of training.
3. Fitted a regression of the Genant grade on the signed distance to the decision boundary,
   calibrated with the mean distance of grade 0 and grade 3 only; rounded to grades.
4. Moved the latent along the normal of the boundary and decoded, to show a vertebra at each grade.

## Results (Table 2 of the paper)

| Model | Detection, area under the curve | Grading, macro F1 |
|---|---|---|
| Support vector machine on frozen latent, trained directly on grades 0, 2, 3 | 0.93 | 0.59 |
| Linear layer on frozen latent, distance regression | 0.96 | 0.44 |
| Support vector machine on frozen latent, distance regression | 0.93 | 0.51 |
| Same, third-degree polynomial calibration (grades 0, 2, 3) | 0.93 | 0.56 |
| 2D DenseNet121 trained end to end | 0.98 | 0.65 |

220 vertebrae carried a grade (74 grade 1, 102 grade 2, 44 grade 3). A 3D comparison
(0.99 / 0.86) was measured on a different test set, as the authors state.

## Where it supports our plan

1. Severity score from a classifier fitted on the extreme grades only, intermediate grades used
   only for evaluation: the same design as our charter rule 6 (score fitted on grade 0 versus 3).
2. A model trained end to end beat the linear model on the frozen latent, most clearly for
   grading. This supports Hugo's ResNet-18 baseline as the reference for what supervised training
   reaches.
3. The polynomial calibration did better than the linear one: the relation between distance and
   severity need not be linear. For us: report the distance per grade and do not assume equal
   steps between grades.
4. The latent grouped vertebrae by spine level in a t-SNE plot, and one counterfactual changed a
   neighbouring vertebra instead of the target one. Same risk for us: orchard, fruit shape or stalk
   instead of browning. Supports the orchard check and the size descriptors.

## Where it contradicts the paper's own framing

The regression on the distance to the boundary (macro F1 0.44 to 0.56) did not beat a support
vector machine trained directly on grades 0, 2 and 3 on the same latent (0.59). The distance score
is interpretable and needs only binary labels; it was not the more accurate grader. For us: report
the severity score beside a direct ordinal model on the same features, not instead of it.

## Where it does not transfer

1. 2D slices of 96 × 96 against our 3D 128³ volumes; about 12,000 slices against our 433 fruit.
2. Their latent is a 512-value vector from a model built to give a meaningful representation; ours
   is a 32³ compression latent with a KL weight of 1e-6. A step along a straight direction in our
   latent may give codes the decoder never saw during training, so decoded counterfactuals may show
   artefacts unrelated to browning.
3. Their decoder is a diffusion model conditioned on a second, stochastic latent that keeps the
   fine detail; Hugo's decoder is a deterministic VAE-GAN decoder.
4. A fracture is a change of shape (height loss); browning is a small density change in the core.
5. The main text does not say whether slices of one patient were kept on one side of the split.
   Their numbers are therefore not a reference for ours, where splits are by orchard.
6. The counterfactual figures are selected examples; the authors show two badly calibrated cases
   themselves.

## Key takeaway

The method is the one we plan for the counterfactuals and the severity score. The paper shows it
works for detection, is weaker for grading than an end-to-end network, and can attribute severity
to the wrong structure. All three points become checks in our design, not assumptions.
